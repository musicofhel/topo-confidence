# Triage brief — 2606.04212 — Edge of Stability Selectively Shapes Learning Across the Data Distribution

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04212
Triaged: 2026-06-06

## Refutations

**Refutation 1 — F-2 (L19 prefill DoM predicts correctness at AUROC 0.7731): DoM direction may be a training-time EoS directional imprint, not an inference-time confidence computation.**

The paper demonstrates that the top Hessian eigenvector v1 rotates during training as different data subsets gain or lose alignment with it (§3.3, Figure 5). When a group's gradient saturates, v1 rotates toward the next-most-aligned subset. If the L19 residual stream encodes a directional shadow of v1 at the point training dynamics froze this orientation, then the DoM direction (cos(DoM, PC1) = 0.9216) could be a training artifact — it captures "how strongly this input was on the EoS fast-track during pretraining" rather than "how confident the model is at inference time." Falsifiable: if DoM is an EoS artifact, it should rotate across models pretrained with different learning rates or different EoS regimes. Comparing DoM directions from Qwen-2.5-1.5B vs. Qwen-2.5-7B (which we already have; cos(prefill_DoM, final_DoM) = 0.046) already shows the direction is not scale-stable within a single model (prefill vs final), but cross-model comparison of the *prefill* DoM direction specifically is needed.

**Refutation 2 — F-4 (Correct trajectories collapse harder, "asymmetric collapse"): Gradient saturation under CE provides a non-confidence explanation.**

The paper (§4.2, Lemma 5) shows that under cross-entropy loss, confidently-classified examples experience gradient saturation: their gradient norms collapse to near zero, decoupling them from v1. At inference time, these "optimization-finished" examples would have representations that are more compressed because they were no longer being actively shaped by late-training dynamics. This offers an alternative to the F-4 narrative ("correct answers collapse because the model is confident at inference time"): correct examples collapse harder because they were *trained to saturation* earlier, and their representations froze in a compressed state. Falsifiable: if the asymmetric collapse is a training artifact, its magnitude should correlate with training-time CE confidence (approximated by output probability on the training distribution) rather than with inference-time problem difficulty. We could probe this by checking whether asymmetric collapse is stronger for "easy" correct problems (likely saturated early in training) vs "hard" correct problems (likely saturated late or not at all).

**Refutation 3 — F-7 (D-bucket has a distinctive collective geometric signature): The D-bucket signature may be a training-time prototype category artifact.**

The paper's prototype taxonomy (§2.3) partitions data by centroid distance and label consistency — properties that are model-independent. If MATH-500 problems sort into analogous geometric categories in L19 activation space (with the D-bucket mapping to "boundary" or "input-outlier" examples), then the distinctive D-bucket geometric signature might reflect where these problems sat in the EoS learning allocation — not an intrinsic property of problem difficulty. The paper's Figure 8 shows that centroid distance in pixel space predicts per-example alignment with v1 at EoS (Spearman ρ = 0.39); if centroid distance in L19 activation space similarly predicts DoM projection strength, then the D-bucket's geometric distinctiveness could be a downstream consequence of EoS-selective training, not a useful confidence signal. Falsifiable: compute centroid distance in L19 activation space for all 500 MATH-500 problems and check whether D-bucket membership is predicted by centroid distance. If yes, the D-bucket signature is likely a geometric rather than semantic property.

## Direct connections to F-N / H-N

- **F-2 (AUROC 0.7731):** The paper's directional-coupling metric cos²θ_k = (∇ℓ_k · v1)² / ‖∇ℓ_k‖² is structurally analogous to our DoM projection — both identify a single direction that carries the dominant signal. Our cos(DoM, PC1) = 0.9216 implies DoM is essentially PC1 of the covariance; their v1 is PC1 of the *Hessian*. The connection is: if the dominant covariance direction (PC1) at inference time is shaped by which data subset dominated v1 during training, then our DoM ≈ PC1 finding is a downstream consequence of EoS-selective learning.

- **F-4 (asymmetric collapse):** Paper's gradient saturation mechanism (§4.2, Figure 7 bottom row) provides a training-time mechanistic explanation: correctly-classified examples have ‖r_i‖ → 0 under CE, so their representation is frozen once they saturate. The "harder collapse" at inference time may reflect this training-time freeze rather than an active inference-time compression.

- **F-7 (D-bucket geometry):** The four-group prototype taxonomy (inlier, boundary, input-outlier, output-outlier) maps onto our difficulty buckets. The key prediction is that centroid distance in activation space should correlate with DoM projection strength, mediated by the paper's alignment×persistence mechanism.

- **F-10 (PH null vs Gaussian):** Already connected via 2604.19740 (EoS ↔ Sharpness Dimension). This paper adds that EoS is *directional*, not just scalar sharpness. The PH null could reflect the fact that EoS concentrates all non-Gaussian structure along v1 and its immediate neighbors, making the full activation distribution appear isotropically Gaussian to topology-based tests that don't privilege v1.

- **H-4 (Breathing ↔ Sharpness Dimension):** The paper's framework provides a more specific version: the "breathing" pattern (dimensional expansion then collapse) could track the rotation of v1 through successive data subsets during inference — analogous to how v1 rotates through successive prototype groups during training (Figure 5). This gives H-4 a mechanistic grounding.

- **H-5 (Prefill signal encodes problem familiarity vs decomposability):** The paper's centroid-distance → alignment finding (ρ = 0.39) directly supports H-5: "familiar" problems (training inliers) would have low centroid distance and low DoM projection, while "unfamiliar" problems (training outliers) would have high centroid distance and potentially high DoM projection. This would mean the prefill signal encodes training-distribution typicality, which is a specific version of "familiarity."

## Methodologies extracted

- **Branching intervention at EoS onset** — fork training trajectory at the moment λ_max(H) reaches 2/η, continue one branch at η (EoS) and drop the other to η/2 (exit). Causal identification of EoS effects on per-subset learning.
  *Replication cost*: ~10min CPU per MLP run (their setup); ~hours for LLM-scale.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — requires training from scratch, and our pipeline is inference-only on frozen Qwen models.

- **Prototype taxonomy** — partition data into inlier/boundary/input-outlier/output-outlier by centroid distance and k-NN label ambiguity (k=50), independent of trained model.
  *Replication cost*: 10min CPU on cached activations.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — directly applicable to L19 prefill activations (500 × 1536) as an unsupervised partitioning that doesn't use correctness labels. Comparing this partition to our correctness-based D-bucket partition is cheap and informative.

- **Directional coupling metric** cos²θ_k = (∇ℓ_k · v1)² / ‖∇ℓ_k‖² — measures how strongly a group's gradient aligns with the top Hessian eigenvector.
  *Replication cost*: requires training gradients (not available for frozen models).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — we don't have access to training gradients.

- **Curvature influence metric** (∇ℓ_k · v1)² = ‖∇ℓ_k‖² · cos²θ_k — factorizes into alignment × magnitude.
  *Replication cost*: same as above (needs training gradients).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — but the factorization *idea* (decomposing a directional signal into alignment and magnitude) is directly applicable to our DoM projections. We can compute (h_i · DoM)² = ‖h_i‖² · cos²(h_i, DoM) for each example and check which factor drives the correctness signal.

- **Centroid-distance → per-example alignment correlation** — Spearman ρ between ‖x_i − μ_c‖ and cos²(∇ℓ_i, v1) at different training stages.
  *Replication cost*: 5min CPU on cached activations (replacing training gradients with DoM projections).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — compute centroid distance in L19 activation space and correlate with DoM projection.

## Approaches & framings

- **EoS as inductive bias, not regularizer.** The paper reframes EoS from "keeps sharpness low" to "determines which data subsets get optimized." This intersects H-4 by suggesting that breathing/sharpness patterns at inference time are downstream consequences of *which data subsets dominated v1 during training*, not properties of the test input itself.

- **Flatness is directional, not scalar.** Curvature matters only in the direction of v1; perpendicular curvature is irrelevant. This reframes our F-10 (PH null): PH is rotation-invariant and thus blind to the *directional* structure that EoS creates. The null might be a measurement mismatch, not evidence of isotropic Gaussianity.

- **Alignment × persistence as the selector.** Two independently necessary conditions for a data subset to benefit from EoS: (1) aggregate gradient aligns with v1, (2) gradient norm doesn't vanish. This decomposition maps onto our DoM (alignment with a direction) × spectrum (magnitude/spread of eigenvalues) factorization in the cov-spectrum probe (AUROC 0.7928).

- Code: extends https://github.com/arseniqum/edge-of-stochastic-stability (Apache 2.0)
- Architecture: 2-hidden-layer MLP (width 512, ReLU), also CNN (3 conv layers, 64-64-128) and BN-free ResNet-14
- Training: full-batch GD, η=0.01, MSE (default) or CE, 10,000 steps
- Top Hessian eigenvalue: via Hessian-vector products, logged every 32 steps
- Prototype construction: m=25 per class per group, α=3 or α=10 for input-outlier displacement
- Hardware: single NVIDIA L40S (48GB), ~15 GPU-hours total
- Branching: fork at t* (first step where λ_max reaches 2/η), exit branch halves η
