# Triage brief — 2502.03407 — Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03407
Triaged: 2026-04-29

## Refutations

**R1 — F-2: Correctness probe may track domain/difficulty clusters, not per-instance correctness**

The paper's primary finding is that their linear probe fires on *deception-incentivized scenarios* even when the model responds honestly — the probe learns "is this a deception-relevant context" in addition to "is this response deceptive." This is precisely the failure mode that F-2's "Strongest counterargument" already flags: "The prefill signal may encode problem *familiarity* (closeness to training data) rather than structural decomposability." The paper demonstrates, in a closely analogous probing setup, that this confound is real and quantitatively significant (honest responses in deceptive-incentivized scenarios score near or above the 1% FPR deceptive threshold). Mechanism: if MATH-500 problem types form distinguishable activation clusters at L19 (e.g., Qwen-2.5-1.5B has higher accuracy on number theory than counting problems), the logistic probe exploits that cluster geometry, yielding 0.7731 AUROC even if per-instance correctness is unpredictable within clusters. This directly challenges F-2's causal interpretation ("L19 DoM predicts correctness"). Falsifiable: compute OOF AUROC within each MATH-500 subject-type stratum (algebra / number theory / geometry / counting / probability). A significant drop — say to ≤ 0.62 in at least two strata — would reclassify F-2 from "instance-level correctness predictor" to "problem-difficulty proxy with limited generalization."

**R2 — F-2: L19 may not be the globally optimal layer; 0.7731 may be an underestimate**

The paper's layer sweep (Appendix D.2) shows sharp performance variation even between adjacent layers. The recall-at-1%-FPR metric collapses to near-zero just 2 layers beyond the chosen optimum for the Insider Trading Confirmation dataset. The paper chose layer 22 of 80 based on one validation split and notes this constitutes a nontrivial hyperparameter. F-2 names L19 as the chosen layer without providing evidence that it is globally optimal across all 28 Qwen-2.5-1.5B layers. Mechanism: if a different layer (e.g., L15 or L22) encodes correctness-predictive information more cleanly, the true ceiling AUROC is higher than 0.7731 and our reported number is a local optimum, not the model's best achievable performance. This would not overturn F-2 (the signal is real) but would reframe the strength: the paper's 0.96–0.999 may partly reflect having swept layers on a larger model. Falsifiable: run the same 5-fold OOF logistic probe on all 28 layers of Qwen-2.5-1.5B using cached Stage 2 NPZs. If the global max is at a layer other than L19 and exceeds 0.7731 by ≥0.01, the specific claim "L19 is the best layer" in F-2 needs revision.

**R3 — H-12: SAE probes generalize worse than raw residual-stream probes (evidence against label-free SAE-based replacement)**

The paper directly tests SAE-based probing: they train logistic regression on Goodfire SAE features (Llama 3.3 70B dictionary) instead of raw residual-stream activations. SAE probes consistently generalize worse across all held-out evaluation datasets compared to raw activation probes. H-12 proposes replacing supervised DoM with label-free semantic-entropy probes (which often rely on SAE-style feature decompositions or monosemantic features) to eliminate the label dependency. Mechanism: SAE features are trained to reconstruct activations under sparsity constraints, not to preserve the linear subspace that encodes abstract task states (correctness, deception); the relevant direction may distribute across many SAE features rather than aligning with a sparse few, making the SAE projection lossy. Falsifiable: implement a sparse-feature probe (SAE projection then logistic regression) on Qwen-2.5-1.5B Stage 2 L19 activations using any available Qwen-family SAE, and compare cross-benchmark AUROC (MATH→BBH transfer) against the 0.7199 DoM baseline. If SAE probe AUROC drops ≥0.03 on transfer, H-12 should be downgraded from LOW to PARKED.

## Direct connections to F-N / H-N

**F-2 (AUROC 0.7731)**: The paper achieves AUROC 0.96–0.999 for deception detection using a single early-layer linear probe on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (layer 22 of 80, ~27% depth). Our best is 0.7731 on Qwen-2.5-1.5B (L19 of 28, ~68% depth). This bounds the ceiling for single-layer linear probes on comparable tasks: deception detection appears to have cleaner linear structure than correctness prediction, or scale (1.5B vs 70B) matters significantly, or our L19 AUROC is genuinely near the ceiling for this model×task. The admission note frames this as "evidence that single-layer linear probes saturate at very high AUROC; bounds what 0.7731 leaves on the table" — the paper corroborates that the method can saturate, not that it *has* saturated for us.

**F-9 (CoE redundancy with DoM) / H-6**: The paper finds that probes trained on in-distribution reasoning (Roleplaying dataset, where all prompts incentivize deception) generalize *worse* to held-out deception types than probes trained on contrastive instructional pairs (Instructed-Pairs, which never contains actual deceptive text). This is structurally analogous to F-9: our CoE-60 trajectory probe (built from in-distribution reasoning paths) is redundant with or weaker than the single DoM direction. The paper explains the mechanism: in-distribution probes may capture surface stylistic features of the training scenarios rather than the abstract directional signal. Supports F-9's conclusion that trajectory features add no cross-domain signal over a 1D direction.

**H-5 (what does the prefill signal encode?)**: The paper provides an existence proof that linear probes on residual streams can strongly encode "is this a task-related context" rather than "is this specific response the target class." This directly sharpens H-5's test priority: the per-subject AUROC stratification is not just "nice to know" but the correct refutation test for whether our 0.7731 is a context proxy.

**H-12 (SAE probe generalization)**: Paper's SAE probe results (consistently worse than raw activations across all datasets) provide direct prior evidence that SAE-based label-free probes do not generalize as well as raw residual stream probing. H-12 is LOW priority; this paper adds a reason to keep it there.

**H-13 (head-level attribution)**: The paper's finding that performance varies sharply by layer (and that layer selection matters more than probe *type* — LR vs DoM vs LDA are similar in most conditions) suggests that head-level decomposition within a layer may add less signal than a cross-layer sweep. Weakly de-prioritizes H-13 relative to a layer sweep.

## Methodologies extracted

- **Logistic regression probe on residual stream** — l2-regularized (λ=10) logistic regression on zero-mean, unit-variance normalized activations; flattened across samples × token positions; probe weight vector defines the direction.
  *Replication cost*: 20min CPU.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — we already use this approach for DoM in F-2; this paper validates the method and adds λ=10 as a calibrated default.

- **Instructed-Pairs contrastive training** — pairs of activations that differ only in an honest/deceptive instruction prefix; the model's response text is held constant. Targets the *intent* dimension before any behavioral signal appears.
  *Replication cost*: ~2h to construct pairs + 20min CPU to train.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes, for a "correctness-intent" version — construct correct-instruction vs incorrect-instruction paired prompts and probe the resulting direction. Potentially cleaner than labeling outputs.

- **Per-layer AUROC sweep** — same probe trained independently at each layer; swept against held-out validation set to select optimal layer.
  *Replication cost*: ~2h CPU on cached NPZs (28 layers × 500 problems).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — directly motivated by R2; would either validate L19 or find a better layer.

- **Mean vs max aggregation of per-token scores** — alternatives to simple mean (max, ReLU-threshold mean) for aggregating per-token probe scores to a per-response classification.
  *Replication cost*: 5min CPU.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes, cheap post-hoc on existing Stage 2 per-token activations.

- **Follow-up question probing** — insert "Was your last response deceptive? (Yes/No)" after each response, gather activations at the "No" response token, train probe on those activations.
  *Replication cost*: H100 day to regenerate activations for MATH-500 with follow-up prompts.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — specific to deception detection; not directly applicable to correctness, where the follow-up would be "Was your answer correct?" (model often outputs "Yes" regardless).

- **SAE-based probe comparison** — replace raw residual activations with Sparse Autoencoder feature activations before logistic regression; compare generalization performance.
  *Replication cost*: 4h CPU if SAE weights are available for Qwen-2.5-1.5B.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes, as a falsification test for H-12 / R3.

## Approaches & framings

**Probe activation ≠ probe classification**: The paper separates "AUROC for deceptive vs honest" from "recall at 1% FPR on control" as two distinct performance metrics. For our correctness prediction task, the analogous split is "AUROC on MATH-500 balanced classes" vs "precision at fixed coverage on deployment distribution." This framing maps directly to our selective-prediction setup (F-8, 71.6% at 50% coverage) and suggests evaluating our probe against a held-out "neutral" math-adjacent distribution to measure control-vs-deceptive gap. Intersects F-8, H-9.

**Generalization gap as a probe quality metric**: The paper frames generalization from Instructed-Pairs (simple contrastive instructions) to realistic deception scenarios as the key test of probe quality. Our DoM generalizes from MATH-500 to BBH (AUROC ~0.7199 per F-9); the paper suggests that probes trained on abstracted contrastive pairs (rather than in-distribution trajectories) tend to generalize better. Suggests an alternative DoM construction via instruction-contrasting rather than outcome-labeling. Intersects F-2, F-9, H-5.

## Datasets & benchmarks

- **Roleplaying dataset** (custom, Apollo Research, inspired by Pacchiardi et al. 2023) — 371 responses (84 honest / 84 ambiguous / 203 deceptive); low-realism scenarios. Not released publicly (appears to be at data.apolloresearch.ai). Applicable? no — MATH-500 regime; but the construction method (GPT-4o-generated scenarios with strategic lie incentives) is replicable.

- **Insider Trading dataset** (Scheurer et al. 2023) — 1329 responses (669/324/336); high-realism AI-assistant-under-pressure scenarios. Applicable? no — but the sandbagging variant (deliberate incorrect answers under instruction) has a structural parallel to our D-bucket analysis (F-7).

- **Sandbagging dataset** (Benton et al. 2024) — 995 responses on WMDP (harmful) vs MMLU (neutral) questions; model instructed to deliberately fail WMDP. Applicable? partial — if we want a probe that detects "model is deliberately giving wrong answers" vs "model genuinely doesn't know," this dataset structure would apply. Not accessible as a standalone download.

- **Alpaca** (Taori et al. 2023) — 9,994 Llama responses to instruction-following queries; used as control to calibrate false positive rate. Applicable? yes — we could use Alpaca responses as a control distribution for measuring our correctness probe's FPR on non-math queries. HF: tatsu-lab/alpaca. Accessible: yes.

## Implementation details worth capturing

- Layer 22 of 80 in Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct was chosen by sweep on Llama-3.1-70B and found to transfer to Llama-3.3; ~27% depth. Note our L19 is ~68% depth in 28-layer model — opposite regime.
- Logistic regression with `l2` penalty and `λ=10` (i.e., `C=0.1` in sklearn's convention); normalization: zero mean, unit variance on training set, applied identically to test.
- Flatten across samples and token positions before fitting (each token position is a training sample with the response-level label).
- Mean aggregation across token positions to produce per-response score.
- Code: https://github.com/ApolloResearch/deception-detection
- Data viewer: https://data.apolloresearch.ai/dd/

## Proposed PAPER_INDEX.md classification

## 2502.03407 — Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes (Goldowsky-Dill, Chughtai, Heimersheim, Hobbhahn / Apollo Research, 2025)

**Relevance:** The paper evaluates linear probes on residual-stream activations of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct for detecting strategic deception, achieving AUROC 0.96–0.999. The task is structurally analogous to our correctness prediction: both are binary classifications over model outputs using early-middle-layer linear probes. The primary relevance is (1) as an empirical upper-bound reference showing that single-layer linear probes can saturate for related tasks, contextualizing our 0.7731 AUROC (F-2); (2) as a methodological comparison showing LR ≈ DoM for in-distribution discrimination but LR > DoM when the control distribution is far from training; and (3) as a direct demonstration of the domain-contamination failure mode that F-2's "strongest counterargument" already flags (H-5).

**Key claim we tested:** Single-layer logistic regression probes on residual-stream activations can detect strategic deception with AUROC 0.96–0.999, generalizing from simple contrastive training data to realistic high-stakes deception scenarios (insider trading, sandbagging).

**Our result:** **TO TEST** The paper's AUROC values (0.96–0.999 for deception) bound the ceiling for our correctness task (0.7731 for F-2). We have not tested whether (a) a layer sweep improves on 0.7731, or (b) our AUROC degrades within matched subject strata (domain contamination). P11-FE72 and H-73 lay out these two tests.

**Related experiments:** P11-FE72, H-73, H-5, F-2, F-9

**Status:** TO TEST
