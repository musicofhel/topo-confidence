# Triage brief — 2605.31175 — Towards Efficient LLMs Annealing with Principled Sample Selection

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31175
Triaged: 2026-06-02

## Refutations

**R1 — Against F-2 (DoM ≈ PC1 predicts correctness at AUROC 0.7731): The dominant variance direction may capture optimization rigidity, not correctness signal.**
DiReCT's framework decomposes spectral space into "stiff modes" (high-eigenvalue directions — learned, rigid optimization constraints) and "flat valleys" (low-eigenvalue — plastic, generalization-relevant). Their Theorem 4.4 proves that steps along *flat* eigenvectors increase landscape flatness and contract curvature, improving generalization. If PC1 in L19 activation space (cos(DoM, PC1) = 0.9216) corresponds to a stiff Hessian mode — plausible since highest-variance activation directions often track highest-curvature parameter directions through the Jacobian — then our DoM probe detects "how strongly this input exercises the most constrained optimization dimension." Easy problems would project weakly onto stiff modes (already converged), hard problems project strongly. The AUROC 0.7731 would then be a confound between difficulty and optimization-landscape position, not a meaningful correctness geometry. **Test:** Partition our cached PCA eigenvalues by spectral elbow into stiff/flat sets; train correctness probes on each partition separately. If stiff-mode PCs carry >80% of the AUROC, the confound hypothesis strengthens.

**R2 — Against the cov-spectrum probe (AUROC 0.7928, linked to F-2): Eigenvalue features may reflect optimization structure, not intrinsic geometry.**
Our cov-spectrum probe uses top-20 log-eigenvalues (PC1-residualized) as features. DiReCT shows that eigenvalue spectra of the loss Hessian directly encode the optimization landscape's curvature profile. If the activation-space eigenvalue spectrum is coupled to the parameter-space Hessian spectrum (via the network Jacobian), then our 0.7928 AUROC may be reading the Hessian stiffness profile rather than correctness-relevant geometric structure. The probe would then be brittle to retraining: different random seeds → different Hessian spectra → broken features. **Test:** Compare eigenvalue spectra from two independently trained Qwen-2.5-1.5B checkpoints on the same MATH-500 inputs. If spectra diverge substantially while DoM AUROC is stable, the confound is real.

**R3 — Against H-743 (L19 spectral gap is an outlier explaining DoM ≈ PC1): Spectral gap may be universal across layers, not L19-specific.**
DiReCT's spectral elbow detection finds k=34 dimensions capture 94.5% of cumulative energy for GPT-2-Medium, and their framework implies this spectral concentration is a convergence property of the loss landscape, not a layer-specific phenomenon. If every Qwen-2.5-1.5B layer has a similar spectral gap (i.e., a small number of PCs capturing >90% of variance), then L19's spectral gap is not special — and H-743's explanation for why DoM ≈ PC1 at L19 (but not elsewhere) collapses. L19 would be special for some other reason (depth in the network, proximity to some representational transition), with the spectral gap being incidental. **Test:** Compute per-layer spectral gap ratios (λ₁/λ₂ and cumulative energy at elbow-k) across all 28 layers of Qwen-2.5-1.5B on cached prefill activations; plot and check whether L19 is actually an outlier.

**R4 — Against H-734 (DoM aligns with top Hessian eigenvector): DiReCT's framework predicts the alignment, but reframes it as uninformative for generalization.**
If H-734 is confirmed (DoM direction does align with the top Hessian eigenvector), DiReCT's Theorem 4.4 says this is precisely the direction you should NOT step along for generalization improvement. The alignment would be true but vacuous — DoM would detect the "rigid backbone" of the optimization, not a direction that tells you about generalization capacity or correctness. This doesn't refute H-734 directly but refutes the *implied significance*: alignment with the top Hessian eigenvector would be a negative indicator under DiReCT's framework, not a positive one. **Test:** If H-734 is eventually confirmed via Hessian computation, check whether flat-mode projections give better correctness AUROC than stiff-mode projections.

## Methodologies extracted

- **Randomized Hessian sketching** — Project d-dimensional gradients through random matrix R ∈ ℝ^{k×d} to get k-dimensional sketches z_x = R∇ℓ(x;θ); eigendecompose the k×k sketched Hessian H̃ = (1/M)Σ z_x z_x^T. Reduces O(d²) Hessian to O(k²).
  *Replication cost*: ~2h H100 (needs fresh backward passes through Qwen-2.5-1.5B for gradient collection).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — requires backward passes we can't do on 2060 Super; relevant only if H-734 becomes high priority.

- **Spectral elbow partitioning (stiff/flat mode split)** — Compute cumulative eigenvalue energy, find the "elbow" k where curve flattens. Indices 1..k are stiff modes; k+1..d are flat. Threshold ε is the eigenvalue at the elbow.
  *Replication cost*: 5min CPU on cached covariance eigenvalues.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — we already have PCA eigenvalues from the 500×1536 L19 activation matrix. Apply elbow detection, partition PCs, train separate probes.

- **Gradient projection scoring** — For each sample, compute per-eigenmode gradient projections g_{i,j} = ⟨∇ℓ(x_i), v_j⟩. Score samples by their energy distribution across stiff vs flat modes.
  *Replication cost*: ~2h H100 (needs gradients).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — same backward-pass barrier.

- **Successive Convex Approximation (SCA) for sample weighting** — Iterative LP relaxation to solve non-convex sample-weight optimization. Converges in ~20 iterations.
  *Replication cost*: 10min CPU (once you have the gradient projections).
  *We'd plausibly run this*: no — blocked by gradient collection.

- **PAC-Bayesian generalization bound with curvature contraction** — Theorem C.5 bounds generalization gap using Tr(H) contraction after flat-direction steps. Gives theoretical justification for flatness-seeking.
  *Replication cost*: Pure theory; can cite, no compute needed.
  *We'd plausibly run this*: yes — could cite the bound when interpreting our cov-spectrum probe's eigenvalue features.

## Approaches & framings

- **Stiff/flat mode decomposition of activation spectra.** The paper frames spectral space as a binary partition: high-eigenvalue "stiff modes" (structural constraints learned during pretraining) vs low-eigenvalue "flat valleys" (plastic, generalization-relevant). This intersects F-2: if DoM ≈ PC1 is a stiff mode, the correctness probe reads optimization structure. If it's a flat mode, it reads generalization capacity.

- **Curvature-aware descent as an alternative to feature-based prediction.** DiReCT reframes sample quality as "does this sample's gradient point in a useful optimization direction?" rather than "does this sample have unusual features?" This is orthogonal to our activation-geometry probing approach, but suggests a complementary signal source: per-sample gradient alignment could be a correctness predictor independent of activation geometry.

- **Spectral elbow as a universal convergence signature.** The paper treats spectral concentration (few modes capturing most energy) as a convergence indicator. This could reframe F-1's "dimensional breathing" as a spectral-concentration trajectory rather than a geometric expansion/collapse phenomenon.

## Implementation details worth capturing

- Spectral elbow detection: k=34 captures 94.5% cumulative energy for GPT-2-Medium (355M params). Scale-dependent — expect different k for Qwen-2.5-1.5B.
- Stiff gradient budget τ_⊥ = 0.1 (constraint on gradient energy in stiff directions).
- SCA converges in ~20 iterations with tolerance δ = 10⁻⁴.
- Annealing hyperparameters: 5 epochs, batch 4, LR 10⁻⁵, weight decay 0.01, cosine scheduler with 0.1 warmup ratio.
- High-loss samples preferentially selected (Pearson r = +0.811 between selection weight and pretraining loss). Interpretation: low-loss = already converged = uninformative gradient.
- Sequence length preference: selected samples concentrate near 125 tokens; shorter sequences largely excluded.
